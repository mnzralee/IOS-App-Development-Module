2. Overview

ParcelCost estimates the cost of posting a parcel. The user enters the parcel’s weight and dimensions and chooses between two services:

  • Personal (Uninsured) — a simple, low-cost service.
  • Business (Insured) — a higher-priced service where the user also chooses an insurance cover amount.

Every successful calculation is saved. A second tab shows the saved history and lets the user filter it by service type.

Tab

  • If any rule fails: show the alert, hide the Results section, and do not save a record.
  • Exactly 30 kg and exactly 150 cm are allowed — the limits are “not exceed”.
  • The alert message reads: “Please ensure all dimensions and weights are greater than 0.” followed by “For insured parcels, maximum weight is 30kg and maximum dimension is 150cm.”

6. What must be stored

Each successful calculation is saved so that the History tab can rebuild a row exactly as shown in Figs. 4–6. Study those rows carefully: the information a row displays tells you what the model must contain.

Two supporting rules:

  • The service type is one of exactly two values, “Personal” or “Business”, and must not be a free-text string.
  • Insurance applies to Business parcels only. A Personal record has no insurance value — it is not “£0”.

Deciding the exact properties and types is your first SwiftData preparation task (Section 7.3).

10. Pause-and-predict questions

Try to answer these before the code appears on screen. Short answers are fine.

  1. Why are the calculator inputs @State (or @AppStorage) rather than properties of the SwiftData model?
  2. Why does a TextField that edits a number need a numeric binding such as Double, and what does the format argument do?
  3. Why does a Toggle or Slider take a binding ($) rather than a plain value?
  4. Why must the insurance slider be inside an if, and what would be wrong with hiding it by setting its opacity to 0?
  5. Why is validation done before the cost is calculated and saved, not after?
  6. Why does the model need a timestamp, and why is a Date better than a string?
  7. Why is @Query a good fit for History compared with copying records into an array of your own?
  8. Why can filtering be a computed property instead of a second stored array?
  9. Why is insuranceAmount optional in the model, and what would go wrong if Personal records stored 0 instead?
  10. The volume is saved in cm³ but displayed in litres. Where should the conversion happen — when saving or when displaying — and why?

11. Make a genuine attempt to build the application before Week 7 lecture

  • Use the starter project and work through the experiments in Section 8. Implement as much as you can independently, but do not expect to finish the application.
  • At a minimum, attempt to extend the input fields and implement the Personal calculation. If you can, continue with the Business mode, validation, SwiftData model and History features.
  • Compile and run frequently. Keep a brief record of what works, what fails and where you get stuck. Bring your project and questions to the Week 7 lecture.
  • An incomplete attempt is expected. The purpose is to encounter the problems yourself before we solve them together.
  • Do not add MVVM, repositories or service layers. Plain SwiftUI views, state and SwiftData are the target.
  • Do not add networking, authentication, settings or extra screens.
  • Do not optimise the formulas or restructure the code before it works.
  • Do not treat the lecture as a demonstration to watch — build alongside it.

12. Success criteria

By the end of the Week 7 lecture you should be able to explain, in your own words:

  • how @State and bindings drive the calculator UI;
  • how conditional SwiftUI controls are shown from state;
  • how a calculation function turns inputs into a result, and why it is a separate function;
  • how guard is used for validation and early exit;
  • how a SwiftData model represents a saved calculation, and why an optional property is used for insurance;
  • how modelContext inserts and saves a new record;
  • how @Query retrieves records for History; and
  • how a computed property filters the displayed records.

Understand the pattern in advance → build it with support → reproduce it independently.
